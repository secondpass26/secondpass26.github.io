I am a 39-year-old software engineer with two kids. I have spent the past 12 years working primarily with C++. At one point in my career, I wanted to switch to backend development, but I never really had the chance. A few small opportunities came up, but I mostly remained in C++.

So I thought: why not go deep with C++ and become the best C++ programmer I can be?

I recently heard someone describe going deep as being one metre wide but 10,000 metres deep, rather than covering a wide floor. I don't actually know if that analogy makes sense. But after a month of searching for a job, no one has offered me a backend role, while a couple of C++ opportunities have appeared.

Maybe this is a sign that I should invest my effort in becoming an expert. To be honest, it does not feel as if I have much choice. Becoming an expert might be my best chance of landing a job. It is what it is. I just have to suck it up and push through.

After chatting with ChatGPT, I realised that the goal should not merely be to become a C++ expert. It should be to become an excellent systems programmer who happens to be extremely good at C++.

That feels like a much stronger career strategy.

C++ becomes my weapon rather than my entire identity.

My skill stack could eventually look like this:

If I reach that level, I can look beyond HFT. I could apply to fields such as:

HFT and trading → robotics → semiconductors → storage and databases → networking → AI infrastructure → embedded systems → security → game engines → performance engineering.

## A one-year objective

Not:

> Master C++ in one year.

Instead:

> Build an excellent systems-programming foundation over one year.

Something roughly like this:

```text
Months 1–3
C++ fundamentals + DSA
│
├── lifetime
├── RAII
├── smart pointers
├── move/copy
├── STL
└── common algorithms

Months 4–6
Computer systems
│
├── Linux
├── processes
├── virtual memory
├── CPU/cache
├── networking
├── threads
└── concurrency

Months 7–9
Performance C++
│
├── profiling
├── allocators
├── cache locality
├── atomics
├── lock-free concepts
├── SIMD
└── benchmarking

Months 10–12
Large systems
│
├── system design
├── networking projects
├── high-performance server
├── storage/database concepts
└── substantial C++ project
```

I will continue studying DSA throughout the year rather than studying it once and dropping it.

After a year, I probably won't be a world-class C++ expert. That takes years. But I could become dramatically stronger at systems programming than the average application developer. I would also have a foundation from which HFT-level expertise becomes more realistic.

The part I like most about this direction is that I don't need to end up in HFT for the investment to pay off. HFT can simply be the benchmark:

> Could I become technically strong enough to pass an HFT C++ interview?

Even if I ultimately choose robotics, infrastructure, storage, AI systems, or another field, that is still a useful mountain to train for.
