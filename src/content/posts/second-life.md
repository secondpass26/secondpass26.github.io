When I was a kid, I remember watching a Jackie Chan movie called *Who Am I?* To be honest, I don't remember much of the movie, but the title stuck in my head: Who am I?

So, who am I? What led to the creation of this blog?

This December, I turn 39. I recently lost my job as a software engineer. At home, there is my wife and our two kids. One is turning one, and the other is turning three this year.

Losing a job is a first for me, so there is plenty of uncertainty. The expected reaction was nervousness and shock. Instead, at least initially, there was a strange sense of excitement.

The severance should tide us over for a couple of months. That gives me some time to rethink my life. Call it a midlife crisis, but a break feels necessary.

For a while, life has felt like a loop, especially since the birth of my second kid. Day after day passes without much of a goal. My weight is the highest it has ever been, motivation is low, and too much time disappears into social media and endless scrolling. A brain-rot situation, as people might call it.

Now there is a little time to rebuild my body and, more importantly, my brain. This blog is where I want to document that process.

Writing used to be a regular habit, usually in the form of blog posts. Blogging may not be as popular anymore, and the thought of people knowing exactly what is in my head still makes me uncomfortable. That is why this blog is anonymous.

If you happen to come across it, welcome. No real audience is expected. This is mostly a place for reflection and, perhaps, a source of motivation.

Maybe it helps to treat life like a movie. That is why the blog is called Second Pass. It feels like being reborn and getting another attempt at living.

## Where things stand

A couple of job applications have gone out, but only one company has responded with an interview. The good news is that the interview seemed to go quite well, and an offer might be coming.

The unknown part is the salary. Hopefully, they will not lowball me.

The job itself sounds interesting, although taking it would require a jump in skills. Most of my experience is in C++, but calling myself an expert would be a stretch. Decent at best.

Data structures and algorithms are a particular weakness. Let's be real: in 12 years of work, I have rarely needed to implement them directly. That gap has always contributed to a sense of impostor syndrome.

With the extra time available now, getting better at them feels worthwhile. They could become part of the foundation that has been missing from my skill set.

It reminds me of martial arts. People practise the same kicks and punches for years. The basics never stop being important. Maybe data structures and algorithms can serve the same role in my programming career.

## A plan for getting better

I asked ChatGPT what it would take to become a “superstar programmer.” The answer covered almost everything: data structures and algorithms, deep C++ knowledge, computer systems, software design, debugging, reading good code, building difficult projects, and learning to explain technical ideas clearly.

It was a useful list, but also an overwhelming one. Trying to improve everything at once would probably become another form of distraction.

What stood out most was the need for a stronger foundation. For me, that means two things first: problem-solving through data structures and algorithms, and a deeper understanding of C++.

The goal with DSA is not to memorise solutions for interviews. It is to become better at breaking down problems, recognising common structures, and reasoning about complexity. There is a lot here that still feels unfamiliar, which is exactly why it needs attention.

C++ is different. It has been part of my career for years, but there are still gaps beneath the surface. Object lifetime, memory, templates, concurrency, undefined behaviour, and what the machine is actually doing all deserve a closer look. Using a language for a long time is not the same as understanding it deeply.

Later, the plan can expand into systems fundamentals, software design, debugging, and larger projects. Building something substantial, then returning to improve the bad decisions in the first version, sounds especially useful. That kind of second pass is probably where much of the real learning happens.

For the next three or four months, though, the focus will stay narrow: DSA problem-solving and C++. Then I will see how it goes.

That is already enough for a start.
